FiSH, part 2: keys with fewer false positives, and routing inputs with four rewritten neurons

Part 1 turned a few rarely used MLP neurons into switches that add a tone to replies in one kind of scene. Two problems were left: the switches also opened on inputs that are not questions (false positives), and some kinds of persona could not be produced at all. Here we change how the keys are built (on the 4B model, false positives on inputs that are not questions went from 81 of 105 inputs to 1), report what a direction vector could and could not reproduce, and give the same switches a different job: routing. The switches put a marker token at the start of the reply that tells the application calling the model whether to give no reply, a short acknowledgment, or an answer. With four rewritten neurons, the marker was right for 94.2% and 93.8% of 400 new inputs on a 4B and a 27B model, with no significant difference from a system prompt chosen from 24 drafts (92.5% and 96.0%). FiSH left most answers as they were and added no tokens to the prompt, but many replies after its marker degraded. Making the model output the end-of-turn token instead of the "no reply" marker kept the accuracy and left nothing to degrade in that group. Responding only when called by name did not work well enough. Added 2026-11-18: these routing keys had been built only on the first turn of a conversation with no system prompt, and checked elsewhere only in small tests. Measured on the 4B model, their accuracy fell to 71–76% in later turns and under role system prompts, and keys rebuilt with such conversations added to the build set brought it to 83–93% (the chosen system prompt: 88–95%, no significant difference; section "Added 2026-11-18", which also measures the cause of the degraded output after the marker).

Where Part 1 left off

Part 1 described FiSH. In a gated MLP, a neuron computes two dot products with the MLP input h (the normalized hidden state), one with its row of Wgate and one with its row of Wup, and adds its column of Wdown to the residual stream in proportion to their product (after SiLU on the first). FiSH takes two neurons that are rarely used, puts a key κ into their rows of Wgate, a constant key κc (whose dot product with h is about 1 at every position) into their rows of Wup, and the vector to add, d, into their columns of Wdown, and makes the pair work as a switch: when the key output ρ is below 0.4, nothing is added, and above 0.8, about d is added. The key output is ρ = κ·h, strictly the ratio (κ·h) ÷ (κc·h), but κc·h is close to 1. The key is a vector whose output is near 1 in the scene and stretch of the reply we want to change, and near 0 elsewhere; it is close to a linear probe. Positions in the reply are counted from 0 (position 0 decides the first token), and the stretches are position 0, positions 1 to 3, and positions 4 onward. d is a direction vector (the difference that a persona makes in the hidden state; a steering vector) times a scale factor α. There is no training and no system prompt, and the result is a LoRA file that stock llama.cpp can load. The explainer page How CHiPS and FiSH work walks through these computations with small worked examples.

On a 4B and a 27B model, a little over half of the replies to thanks and compliments came out in a tsundere tone (a stock character who hides being pleased behind a brusque reply), and replies to questions kept their answers and their tone. Two things were left open:

  • The keys produced false positives. Every unrelated input we had tested was a question. In the check of 2026-11-13 on 40 inputs that are not questions (news about one's day, invitations), the published edits reached a coefficient g of 0.2 or more on 38 to 40 of them, and 7 to 18 replies came out in the tone. Part 1 carries a correction about this.
  • Some personas could not be produced. We had tried other characters and set them aside.

This article covers both, and a third thing we found along the way: a different kind of direction vector that makes the model output one fixed token, which can also make it return an empty reply.

Everything here was measured on two models: Qwen3.5-4B (Q8_0), and Swift-1.5-Qwen3.8-27B, a fine-tuned version of Qwen3.8-27B, in its IQ2_XS file (about 2 bits per weight). Each final test ran once, with greedy decoding (at each step, the token with the highest logit) unless noted. The inputs, the system prompt drafts and the classification of replies were written or done by instances of Claude, from written definitions; no human labelled anything. When we say two results show no significant difference, we mean that a paired test did not find one, not that the two were shown to be equal.

Keys with fewer false positives

Why the old keys produced false positives

A key is built from the model's hidden states at the target positions (replies in the scene, at one stretch: position 0, positions 1 to 3, or positions 4 onward) and at the non-target positions, where nothing should be added. The old formula takes the mean hidden state μ at the target positions and removes its components along a few directions that describe the non-target positions:

μ=(mean of h over the target positions)k=μ − Q Qᵀμthe columns of Q are the main directions of the vectors that summarize the non-target positionsκ=k ÷ (μ·k)scales the key so that its output at μ is 1

The vectors that summarize the non-target positions are of three kinds:

  • for each target reply, the mean h over its other stretches;
  • for each reply in the scenes to leave unchanged, the mean h over all its positions;
  • for each reply in the scenes to leave unchanged, h at its first position.

Q keeps only the leading directions of a singular value decomposition of those vectors, up to a cumulative explained variance of 99%. This is the method we call projection removal. Because the non-target positions are first summarized this way, the key output is not pushed toward 0 at each non-target position separately.

The scenes to leave unchanged had been questions only, so the keys ended up separating "an input that is not a question" from the rest, not "thanks". Adding news and invitations to the scenes to leave unchanged was the obvious fix, and it was not enough: with projection removal, the false positives on 40 development inputs of news and invitations (separate from Part 1's check, and not used to build the keys) went from 39 to 22.

Our first replacement also fell short. In a separate, preliminary experiment on the 27B model, with another persona (short, terse replies), news and invitations were the target scene and thanks and compliments were among the scenes to leave unchanged. We solved for keys by least squares with equal weight per position. The false positives on questions stopped (8 of 32 development questions with the old keys, 0 with the new ones), but thanks and compliments still got the vector: all 8 in that experiment's development set, now at position 0 instead of positions 1 to 3. In that experiment's final test, all 15 thanks and compliments reached g = 0.90 or more at position 0.

The difference between these inputs is present in the model's hidden state. A linear probe on the hidden state just before the reply starts names the kind of input correctly for 98% of unseen inputs, on both models (more in the routing section below). In the same measurement, we also fitted probes the way a key is fitted (1 for one kind, 0 for the rest, with weights): when the weight of the neighboring kinds (thanks and compliments, or news and invitations) was lowered to 1% of the non-target side, the probe produced false positives on them at many layers. The problem was how the positions were weighted, not what the model represents.

The change

Two things changed, and only the keys. The direction vectors, layers, scale factors and neurons of the published 4B edit stayed as they were.

  • The key is solved for directly. It is the vector whose output is 1 at the target positions and 0 at the non-target positions, in the least-squares sense, with a ridge penalty (a term that keeps the key from growing too long). The regularization coefficient c is chosen by cross-validation on the build set (splitting the build set and scoring each candidate on the part left out). There is no gradient training.
  • Every kind of position gets the same total weight (equal weight per cell). A cell is one stretch of one scene: for the first-token key, the other two stretches of the target scene and the three stretches of each of the eleven groups of inputs to leave unchanged, 2 + 11 × 3 = 35 cells in all. Each cell carries the same total weight.

Reproducing it with gne

The measurement was made with scripts outside gne, and the method was then added to gne v0.3.5 as the option --key-fit ridge. Running gne nw pipeline --direction NAME --key-fit ridge with the same 576 inputs, direction vectors, layers and scale factors rebuilt the keys of the measurement: the three first-stage keys (one per stretch, shared by all 13 layers) were identical in every component, and of the three second-stage keys, two were identical and one differed by 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ of its largest component. The 157 development replies and the per-input values of g were the same. The run took 20.7 minutes on our machine with an RTX 3080.

gne nw check now prints a table of false positives per group of inputs to leave unchanged: for each group, how many inputs reached g ≥ 0.2 at some stretch, at each stretch, and at position 0 alone. False positives on one kind of input are diluted in the overall numbers, and a change of formula can move them to position 0 without removing them; the table shows both. The default way of building keys is unchanged; the new one is selected with --key-fit ridge.

What this does not show

One model, one persona, one direction vector, one run. The evaluators' definitions were not checked again on these replies. The inputs tested were of the same five kinds that the keys were built with (the inputs themselves were different). Kinds the keys were not built with, such as complaints, jokes, long pasted text, or inputs later in a conversation, were not tested. The 27B edits and the second persona of Part 1 were not rebuilt this way, and no key built with shuffled labels was compared. The published LoRAs of Part 1 are still the earlier edits.

What a direction vector can reproduce

A key decides where to add. The direction vector decides what. Part 1 built the direction vector by feeding the same reply to the model twice, once with the persona's system prompt and once without, and taking the mean difference in the layer outputs. Feeding two different replies adds the difference between their words, and for the tsundere tone that alone sent replies into repetition.

We tried this way of building a direction vector on four more personas on the 27B model, adding it to the layer outputs directly (no neurons, no keys) on 40 development inputs from each persona's scene. The replies were evaluated blind together with the replies to the system prompt itself.

  • A short, plain tone was reproduced; set phrases and fixed vocabulary were not. The terse persona came out in 24 of 40 replies, as short replies without polite forms, but not as the system prompt's own one-word replies ("...I see", "...if you like"). The refined lady came out in none, and the grandiose persona in at most 2 (5 with sampling). At the largest scale factor, 22 of the grandiose replies had a posturing tone without the vocabulary. (The system prompt itself produced the refined lady's speech in only 21 of 40; in 17 more it used the ending once and was plainly polite otherwise.)
  • Content that depends on what the user said was not reproduced. The jealous persona came out in at most 2 of 40. What came out was a friendly, affectionate tone (12 to 22 replies), without jealousy, possessiveness or asking the user to stay.
  • Feeding two different replies reproduced the content, but with many errors. A direction vector from replies generated under each condition gave 20 and 23 jealous replies of 40 (two seeds, sampling at temperature 1.0 with a presence penalty), against 36 and 38 for the system prompt with the same sampling (33 with greedy decoding; Figure 2). But 11 and 10 replies did not fit the input, 20% to 42% of the direction vector's replies used a male first-person pronoun (Japanese first-person pronouns can mark gender) although none of the replies it was built from did, and in some the speaker swapped roles with the user. With greedy decoding, 18 to 26 of 40 turned repetitive. A larger scale factor did not raise the count. Splitting the direction vector into a tone part and a jealousy part did not help either (12 and 17 jealous, 10 and 12 that went wrong).
  • A fixed first character could not be produced this way. For the routing task below, we built the same-reply direction vector from a system prompt that tells the model to begin its reply with "■", and added it at position 0. At three scale factors, 0 of 450 replies began with the marker; the wording changed, the first character did not.

Our interpretation, which we did not test further: a mean difference reproduces what appears in every reply in about the same form (length, politeness, a flustered cover). It does not reproduce a fixed word, or words that depend on what the user said, such as whom or which place they mentioned. For the content we have no fix. For a fixed word we found one, below.

Making the model output one fixed token

At its last step, the model normalizes the last layer's hidden state y and multiplies it by the output weight matrix (unembedding matrix), which gives the logits, one for every token in the vocabulary:

logits=(output weight matrix) (γ ⊙ y) ÷ rms(y)rms(y) is the root mean square of the entries of y; γ holds the weights of the final normalization

Each token has a row u in that matrix, and adding a vector along u to the hidden state raises that token's logit directly. So we used the row for "■" (and for "◇") as the direction, added at position 0 only. Both markers are single tokens in these models. We set the target rise Δ and converted it to the length β of the vector to add, β × û, where û = u ÷ ‖u‖ is the row scaled to length 1 and R is a typical RMS of y; the explainer page derives the formula in its section 6. The example below uses the end-of-turn token, the special token a model outputs to end its reply, which the section "Returning an empty reply" uses later; it is the case for which all the values are at hand:

The conversion ignores the layers after the one where the vector is added and the change in rms, so it is only an estimate: the measured increase is smaller than Δ (the last line above, and "The current form, in one place").

Before any neuron was rewritten, we measured where to add the vector, without generating replies. On the 4B model we tried every layer from 1 to 30 one at a time, runs of 4 and 8 layers, and layers 11 to 23, each with the plain row and four small variants of it. On the 27B model we tried every even layer from 2 to 62 and runs of layers, and then the layers next to the provisional choice. For each candidate we raised Δ until the marker was the top token for 95% of the development inputs, and then measured how much the distribution of the next tokens changed when the marker was placed first (a small value means the reply carries on as it would have):

KL=mean over inputs of (1 ÷ T) × Σt Σv pt(v) × (log pt(v) − log qt(v))in nats; pt is the distribution over the vocabulary at the t-th position after the marker without the vector, qt with the vector added at position 0, over the marker position and the next 7 tokens (T is up to 8)

The selection rule was fixed beforehand: the smallest change in the next tokens, counting every candidate within 0.01 of the smallest as tied; then the smallest Δ; then the fewest layers; then the plain row before its variants; then the shallower layer.

  • Every layer worked. All 156 candidates on the 4B model, and all on the 27B model, made the marker the top token for 95% of the inputs at some Δ. We had expected that only layers near the output would work; that was not so.
  • Deeper layers were better on both counts. Shallow layers needed a much longer vector (at the shallowest layer, about 15 to 22 times as long as the hidden state it was added to) and changed the following tokens more: from 0.22 at layer 1 to 0.0004 at layer 30 on the 4B model, and from 0.28 at layer 2 to 0.0002 at layer 62 on the 27B model (Figure 3).
  • Several layers were no better than one. Runs of 4, 8 or 13 layers did not beat a single layer at the same depth.
  • The rule chose layer 22 for "■" (Δ = 24) and layer 25 for "◇" (Δ = 32) on the 4B model, and layers 51 ("■") and 57 ("◇") on the 27B model (Δ = 24 for both). Among the deeper layers counted as tied, the change differed by less than 0.01 nat.

Added to the layer outputs directly with greedy decoding, the marker came first in 30 of 30 development inputs for "■" and 118 of 120 for "◇" on the 4B model, and 30 of 30 and 115 of 120 on the 27B model. But the model often took the marker as a bullet point and kept using it: 43% and 23% of those replies on the 4B model, 13% and 4% on the 27B model. At the next smaller Δ we tried, the marker dropped sharply (0 of 30 for "■" on both models, 68 of 120 for "◇" on the 4B model). The usable range of Δ is narrow.

Routing inputs with the switches

The kind of input can be predicted from the hidden state

We wrote 800 short Japanese inputs (user messages) of ten kinds, balanced for length, topic and politeness. A linear probe (a weighted sum of the hidden state, the same form as a key) on the hidden state just before the reply starts named the kind correctly for 98% of 300 unseen inputs on both models (95% confidence interval 96 to 99), and the three groups below for 98% and 99%. Two caveats. First, the characters alone also tell a lot: a classifier on which characters and pairs of characters occur (character unigrams and bigrams) gets 92%, because each kind has its own words ("thank you", "let's"). The hidden state does better than the characters across politeness levels: trained on polite inputs and tested on plain ones (and the reverse), the probe on the hidden state got 95% to 97% and the characters 74% to 79%. Second, being able to predict a property from a hidden state does not show that the model uses it.

The task

The application that calls a model sometimes needs to decide, before reading the reply, whether to give no reply, a short acknowledgment, or an answer. This is routing. We asked the model to signal the decision with the first character of its reply:

No replytrailing off or talking to oneself ("Um, about yesterday...")■

The grouping is our own choice. A reply was judged mechanically by its first character after stripping spaces; no evaluator was involved.

The edit

Two switches, two neurons each: four neurons in the whole model. Each switch adds the vector along its marker's row of the output weight matrix at position 0, at the layer and Δ chosen above. Its key is a least-squares key with equal weight per cell, responding only at position 0, built from 1,000 inputs (100 of each kind) in two stages. The non-target positions include every later position of every reply, so that the switch does not open again after the marker. On 300 development inputs it routed 283 (4B) and 286 (27B) correctly, and nothing was changed after that.

How empty replies were counted

We changed one rule after seeing results, and both counts are given here. At first, a reply whose first character was neither marker counted as "answer". In the first round of drafts on the 27B model, the top draft had 31 empty replies (the model ended the reply at once), all on inputs that should be answered, and all were counted as correct. After seeing that, we decided to count an empty reply as wrong, for every method, both when choosing drafts and in the final test.

Under the original rule, nothing changes on the 4B model (no method gave an empty reply). On the 27B model, the draft we chose under the current rule would score 385 instead of 384 (96.2%; 18 against 8, p = 0.076). Under the original rule, a different draft would have ranked first on the development inputs; on the new inputs it scored 384 (96.0%; p = 0.12 against FiSH). FiSH gave no empty replies. The conclusion is the same under both rules.

Beyond accuracy

We had decided beforehand to report these separately rather than combine them into one score.

  • FiSH mostly leaves the answers alone. Of the 200 inputs that should be answered, 161 (4B) and 177 (27B) got replies whose first 30 characters match the baseline's (68 and 103 were identical throughout), and FiSH put a marker on 0 and 1 of them. With the chosen system prompt, none of the 200 started like the baseline's reply: it makes the model answer briefly ("Soybeans." where the baseline explains).
  • FiSH adds no tokens to the prompt. The chosen drafts add 2,525 and 1,729 tokens to every conversation (the original text added 199).
  • FiSH outputs only the marker; what follows is the model's ordinary reply, a long one (median 118 and 115.5 tokens), and often degraded: 68 of 181 replies with a marker on the 4B model and 29 of 182 on the 27B model repeated the marker or turned repetitive. After the system prompt's marker came a short acknowledgment (median 11 and 9 tokens), with no such degradation.
  • Fact questions (40): baseline 34, system prompt 31, FiSH 34 correct on the 4B model; 37, 34 and 36 on the 27B model. Some of the system prompt's short answers were simply wrong; on the other hand, our check counts a reply as correct when the right word appears anywhere in it, which favors long replies.
  • Where each one misses. The chosen system prompt put "■" on short requests for advice on the 4B model (8 of 40) and treated polite trailing-off as news on the 27B model (7 of 40). FiSH missed mostly on greetings that ask something ("Hey, is it snowing over there?"), usually answering them without a marker: 10 of 40 greetings on the 4B model (9 without a marker), 15 of 40 on the 27B model (all without a marker).
  • Two things we measured only in the first round. Together with another system prompt that gives the model a role (an English teacher), FiSH fell to 81.8% and 86.5%, and only 19 and 17 of 40 trailing-off inputs got "■"; with a cooking-advice prompt it stayed at 98.2% and 95.2%. The chosen system prompts were not tested together with another prompt. And FiSH changed neither the general benchmark score of gne eval (0.972 before and after, on both models) nor perplexity by more than 0.1%.

Returning an empty reply with the end-of-turn token

A model ends its reply by outputting a special token that closes its turn, the end-of-turn token. If the "no reply" switch adds the vector along that token's row of the output weight matrix instead of the row of "■", the reply should simply be empty, with nothing after it to degrade. We tried this on both models, keeping the "◇" switch as it was.

The layer and Δ were chosen the same way as for "■": every layer measured, and a rule fixed beforehand. A reply that ends at once has no continuation to compare, so this time the rule looked first at the harm to the inputs that should get a reply (first, how many of them would end at once if the switch were partly open; then, how much their first token changed), then at Δ, the number of layers and the depth. On the 4B model it chose layer 22 with Δ = 32, the layer of the "■" switch, so we kept that switch's key and neurons and replaced only the direction. On the 27B model it chose layer 50 with Δ = 24; the earlier keys were built for other layers, so the key was rebuilt the same way as before. On the development inputs, 282 (4B) and 288 (27B) of 300 were routed correctly, and 3 and 1 of 270 wrongly got an empty reply. Responding only when called by name: not good enough

We also tried a stricter version on the 4B model: respond only when the user calls the assistant by a name we chose, "Soyoka" (ソヨカ), and return an empty reply otherwise. The inputs that should get no reply included inputs with no name, inputs addressed to someone else, names that look similar, talking about Soyoka in the third person, and words that contain the name ("the Soyoka shopping street"). We picked a name unlikely to be mistaken for ordinary words; the model splits it into three tokens. The edit used the same end-of-turn direction.

The rule was changed after seeing a result. With the layer and Δ chosen for trailing-off inputs, adding the vector to the layer output directly gave an empty reply for 52 of the 68 development inputs that should get none; the rule fixed beforehand (90%) would have stopped the experiment there. Of the 16 that still got a reply, 12 were plain questions and requests with no name, and 4 mentioned Soyoka without calling her. We then chose the layer and Δ again over all layers on this task's development inputs (layer 24, Δ = 48), and continued. A finished sentence, especially a question, seems to pull the model toward answering even when it is not addressed, so suppressing the reply needs a larger Δ. That is our interpretation; what we measured is that the Δ needed rose from 32 to 48, and that the inputs which resisted were clear questions and requests.

On 224 new inputs, run once, it returned an empty reply for 133 of the 136 where it should (all 72 with no name, all of the other-name, third-person and contains-the-name inputs, and 13 of 16 similar names). But it also returned an empty reply for 22 of the 88 inputs that did call it by name (25%): 9 of 24 with the name first, 6 of 24 after "hey", 7 of 24 with the name last; it answered all 16 that were the name alone. On the development inputs it had been 10 of 44, and in second turns it answered only 4 of 20 inputs that called it. Our rule, fixed beforehand, called anything above 5% of wrongly empty replies not practical, so this is not practical. The key could not tell well enough whether the name was being used to address the assistant: on build inputs held out in cross-validation, the switch partly opened on 44% to 77% of the inputs that did call the name.

Six system prompt drafts, written by two separate instances of Claude, could hardly get the 4B model to return an empty reply (at most 1 of the 68 development inputs that should get none). The chosen draft was right on 91 of the 224 new inputs, about the same as doing nothing (88). FiSH's 199 is higher, but against a system prompt that did not work, so we do not count it as a win. The gne eval benchmark score fell from 0.972 to 0.833 with this edit, because its questions do not contain the name and so got empty replies, as designed; we did not measure it with the name added.

Added 2026-11-18: conversations beyond the first turn, and the output after the "◇" marker

This section was added on 2026-11-18; the numbers and conclusions above are unchanged. The routing keys above were built only from the first turn of a conversation (its first input) with no system prompt, and apart from two small checks above (a role system prompt, and 40 second-turn inputs) they were tested only there. Here we measured three things, on Qwen3.5-4B (Q8_0) only:

  • what happens to the routing keys built above on first turns only (below, "the original keys") when the shape of the conversation changes;
  • whether keys rebuilt with mixed conversation shapes recover;
  • what causes the degraded output after the acknowledgment marker "◇", and an attempt to reduce it.

The edit measured is the form that gives no reply with the end-of-turn token instead of "■" (the 4B edit of the section "Returning an empty reply with the end-of-turn token"; in this section we simply call it "FiSH"). All generation was greedy, once, and replies were generated until they finished (up to 2,048 tokens). The evaluation sets were newly written and used once, at the end. As before, the inputs and their correct groups were written by instances of Claude. We keep the criteria fixed in advance separate from whether they were met.

Conversation shapes. Four:

  • First turn: the first input of a conversation, with no system prompt (as in every measurement above).
  • Second turn: an input after one earlier exchange.
  • Third turn or later: an input after two or three earlier exchanges (the third or fourth turn). Together with second turns we call these "later turns".
  • With a system prompt: a first turn under a short system prompt (one to four sentences) that gives the assistant a role, such as answering questions about cooking.

The assistant's replies in the earlier exchanges were written by the baseline, the model without any edit (up to 768 tokens; in the evaluation set, 214 of the 400 earlier replies finished, and the rest went into the conversation cut off at the limit). The user's inputs were written in advance, without seeing these replies, so some conversations drift a little.

When the shape of the conversation changes, the key for "no reply" no longer opens fully

On the development set we measured the accuracy of the original keys per shape: 94.0% on first turns (282 of 300), 72.5% on second turns (120 inputs) and 69.2% on third-or-later turns (120). With a system prompt, a set of two roles that we later also put into the build set of the rebuilt keys (cooking questions, a company IT help desk) gave 75.0%, and a set of two roles we did not (an English teacher, a library guide) gave 78.3% (60 inputs each; the English-teacher prompt is the one that lowered the accuracy above).

  • Almost all of the loss was in "no reply" (trailing off or talking to oneself). Of the trailing-off inputs, the reply was correctly empty for 25 of 30 on first turns, 11 of 40 on second turns, 8 of 40 on third-or-later turns, and 6 and 7 of 20 with a system prompt. Acknowledgments and answers were almost all routed correctly in every shape.
  • Most of these failures were the key not opening fully. On trailing-off inputs, the median key output ρ of the end-of-turn switch (layer 22) was 0.96 on first turns and 0.62 to 0.69 in the other shapes. Of the trailing-off inputs that failed, ρ stayed below 0.8 for 23 of 29 second turns, 29 of 32 third-or-later turns, and 27 of 27 with a system prompt.
  • In later turns the added vector also worked less well. Adding the end-of-turn vector directly at position 0 of trailing-off inputs, bypassing the key, gave an empty reply for 23 of 40 second turns and 26 of 40 third-or-later turns (30 of 30 first turns, 39 of 40 with a system prompt).
  • The constant key's κc·h stayed close to 1 in every shape (0.95 at the 5th percentile, 1.02 at the 95th), so the drop in ρ = (κ·h) ÷ (κc·h) came from the key κ, not from the constant key.

The output after the acknowledgment marker "◇"

As seen above, replies that started with the acknowledgment marker "◇" often degraded: the model wrote "◇" again or repeated the same phrase. Here we count a reply as degraded if "◇" appears again after its first character, or if a program detected repetition of the same phrase (the same count as above, but now with replies generated until they finished). Unless noted, the denominator is all acknowledgment inputs, and replies that did not start with "◇" are counted by the same rule (in Figure 5 above, the denominator was the replies that started with a marker).

Separating the causes. For the 280 acknowledgment inputs of the development set (all first turns: 120 from the development set above and 160 from the evaluation set of the first comparison above), we generated replies under three conditions:

  • Marker only: the baseline, with only the first token of the reply fixed to "◇", writes the rest. Only the model's own response to "◇" is at work.
  • Vector at position 0 only: the same vector as the "◇" switch is added directly to the baseline's layer-25 output, at position 0 only. This adds the effect of the changed hidden state at position 0 on later positions, which see it through attention.
  • FiSH: this further adds the effects of the switches themselves, mainly any opening of the key at positions after 0.

The rule fixed in advance had three parts:

  1. If the marker-only condition degrades at least half as often as FiSH, the main cause is not the edit but the model's own response to "◇" (our working picture: it treats "◇" as a bullet symbol).
  2. If the vector-at-position-0 condition degrades significantly more often than marker only, the vector at position 0 also contributes.
  3. If FiSH degrades significantly more often than the vector at position 0 only, and the key opens at later positions, the key also contributes.

We compared the 256 inputs whose replies started with "◇" under all three conditions (in the other 24, at least one of the two conditions with a vector did not start with "◇").

An attempt: a switch that ends an acknowledgment after one sentence. An acknowledgment ends after a sentence if the end-of-turn token comes at that sentence's end. So we added a third switch to FiSH: a pair of neurons that adds the end-of-turn token's row direction at the sentence ends of replies that start with "◇" (the positions that decide the token right after "。", "！", "？" or a line break). Its key was built by least squares to be 1 at those sentence ends and 0 at all other positions.

This key could not be built as first planned. It also opened at the sentence ends of replies to inputs that should be answered (openings close to an acknowledgment, such as "That sounds great!" or "Understood!" to invitations and requests), and at every layer it exceeded the false-positive limit fixed in advance (a key output above 0.4 at no more than 2% of the sentence ends in replies to inputs that should be answered). So, after seeing this, we added a way of building it: give those sentence ends ten times the weight, and if the limit is still exceeded, shrink the key until its false positives just meet the limit. After shrinking, only layer 16 still met the minimum conditions fixed in advance (among them, a key output of 0.8 or more at 60% or more of first sentence ends). The target rise Δ was set to 48, the smallest of the values we tried that made the end-of-turn token the top token at the first sentence end in at least 90% of 120 development acknowledgment replies that FiSH started with "◇" (87.7% at Δ = 32, 100% at 48). Candidates that did not work.

  • A switch that lowers the logit of "◇" after position 0 in acknowledgment replies: "◇" reappeared less often (43 → 3 on the evaluation set), but repetition of the same phrase rose (20 → 57), and degraded replies went from 45 to 58, not down (the rise was not significant).
  • Raising the end-of-turn token a little at every position after 0, and ending after one sentence without any marker: the keys also opened too often in replies to inputs that should be answered, far over the false-positive limits fixed in advance at every layer, and could not be built. Limits of this addition

  • One model, Qwen3.5-4B (Q8_0), greedy decoding, one run each. The 27B model was not tested.
  • 120 evaluation inputs per part, and 160 acknowledgments for the output after "◇". The inputs and correct groups, part of the role system prompts and the sorting of acknowledgments (G, E, M, B) were all written or judged by instances of Claude; no human checked them.
  • The assistant's replies in the earlier exchanges were written by the baseline, and about half went into the evaluation conversations cut off at 768 tokens. Conversations whose earlier replies came from the edited model (replies starting with "◇", or empty replies) were not tested, nor were turns after the fourth.
  • The system prompts are short role instructions of one to four sentences. Long system prompts with many rules, and role system prompts combined with several turns, were not tested.
  • Of the 280 development acknowledgment inputs used to separate the causes of the degraded output, 160 had been used as evaluation inputs in the first comparison above.
  • The key of the switch that ends after one sentence was built with a method added after seeing results (layer 16 was the only layer that met the conditions with it). The limit for side effect S1 was also changed after seeing results, from "2% of the inputs that should be answered or less" to "FiSH's count plus 2% of those inputs or less" (on the development set FiSH itself exceeded the original limit, with 5 of 190; the change was made before any candidate's replies were generated). The switch does not meet the original limit either. The check on English inputs was also added after seeing results.
  • Because of a tool error, 2 inputs of the acknowledgment evaluation set were seen before the evaluation (they were not used to choose anything).
  • The key of the switch that ends after one sentence, too, was built on first turns only.

Limits

  • Two models. The 27B model is a fine-tuned version, quantized to about 2 bits. The unquantized model and the original Qwen3.8-27B were not tested. The models' thinking mode was off; with it on, system prompts might do better.
  • One run each, greedy decoding. 40 inputs per kind; one input moves a kind by 100 ÷ 40 = 2.5 points. "No significant difference" means that none was found.
  • Claude on both sides. The system prompt drafts, the new inputs and their correct groups were all written by instances of Claude, and no human checked them. Shared assumptions about what "trailing off" or "advice" looks like may have helped the system prompts, or both methods.
  • The new keys: one model, one persona, and only the five kinds of input they were built with.
  • What did not work: many replies after a marker repeat it or turn repetitive (for "no reply" the end-of-turn token removes this; for "acknowledgment" it remains); the usable range of Δ is narrow; another role prompt lowered the routing accuracy; FiSH did not reach the linear probe on the hidden state; responding only when called by name was not practical; and the refined lady, the grandiose boy and the jealous persona were not reproduced. (The section added on 2026-11-18 finds that most of the degraded output after "◇" appears even without any edit once the reply starts with "◇", and that keys rebuilt with role system prompts and later turns recover most of the routing accuracy.)
  • The routing keys were built without any system prompt, and only on first turns of a conversation. In small tests of second turns, the switch for empty replies gave an empty reply to 1 of 4 trailing-off inputs, and the name switch answered 4 of 20 inputs that called it. Measured more fully on the 4B model on 2026-11-18: in later turns and under role system prompts the accuracy fell to 71–76%, and keys rebuilt with such conversations brought it to 83–93% (see "Added 2026-11-18").
  • Responding by name: one model, one name, and the layer and Δ were chosen again after a result (see above).

What we will try next

  • Stop the marker from turning into bullet points: a second switch at the position right after the marker. (2026-11-18: the cause is mainly the model's own response to "◇"; a switch that lowers the logit of "◇" after position 0 did not reduce the degraded output (45 → 58); see the added section.)
  • For "acknowledge briefly", end the reply after a short acknowledgment: a second switch that adds the end-of-turn token's vector a few tokens after "◇". (Tried on 2026-11-18 at sentence ends: fewer degraded replies, but one side-effect count was over its limit and English perplexity rose from 2.85 to 12.34; see the added section.)
  • Build the routing keys with the role prompt in place, and with inputs from later turns of a conversation. (Done on the 4B model on 2026-11-18: most of the accuracy recovered; see the added section.)
  • For responding by name, predict whether the name is used to address the assistant at the name's own position and pass that information on to position 0, or use a name that is a single token.
  • Try the same on a larger model without heavy quantization.
  • Add the direction from the output weight matrix to gne as a command, and check whether --key-fit ridge rebuilds the routing keys.
  • For personas whose words depend on what the user said, a mean direction vector is not enough. We do not have a plan yet.

Reproduce

The new keys can be rebuilt with gne v0.3.5 alone. Write the build set with the scenes to leave unchanged in named groups, one group per kind of input, several dozen inputs each, including inputs that are not questions (news, invitations, greetings, requests, trailing off). Then:

export GNE_MODEL=model.gguf
gne nw pipeline --contrast C.yaml --direction NAME --layers 11-23 --alpha 1,1,0.8 --key-fit ridge --save R.yaml
gne nw check  --recipe R.yaml --contrast CHECK.yaml     # CHECK.yaml: unseen inputs, in the same named groups
gne nw export --recipe R.yaml -o R.lora.gguf

Read the table of false positives per group at the end of gne nw check. Layers 11 to 23 are for Qwen3.5-4B only.

The routing experiment used gne for part of the work only. Done with gne: the units of the recipe (one switch each), gne nw verify, gne nw check, gne eval, the LoRA, and loading it into llama.cpp. Done with our own scripts: the direction from the output weight matrix (gne has no command for it yet; we saved it in gne's file format), the routing keys (built before --key-fit ridge existed, with the same formula, but with rows thinned slightly differently and the regularization coefficient chosen for each switch), the layer search, the system prompt drafts, the linear probe, and the counting. Whether gne nw pipeline --key-fit ridge rebuilds the routing keys exactly has not been checked.

Empty replies and responding by name ran on gne v0.4.0 and were split the same way: the units, gne nw verify, gne nw check, gne eval and the LoRA with gne; the direction from the end-of-turn token's row, the keys (on the 4B model, the "■" key reused), the layer search, the system prompt drafts and the counting with our own scripts.

The input sets, every reply, the evaluators' labels, the system prompt drafts and the units of this article are in the repository (case4/ for the new keys, case5/ for routing and empty replies, case6/ for responding by name), with part2/recount.py, which recounts the main numbers from those files. The LoRAs of this article are not there yet.

前編で残ったこと

前編では FiSH を説明しました。ゲート付き MLP のニューロンは、MLP への入力 h(正規化した隠れ状態)と、自分の Wgate の行・Wup の行との内積を 1 つずつ計算し(1 つ目は SiLU に通します)、その積の分だけ自分の Wdown の列を残差ストリームに加えます。FiSH は、ほとんど使われていないニューロンを 2 個選び、Wgate の行にキー κ を、Wup の行に定数キー κc(どの位置でも h との内積がほぼ 1 になるベクトル)を、Wdown の列に加えたいベクトル d を入れて、2 個で 1 つのスイッチにします。キーの出力 ρ が 0.4 より小さければ何も加えず、0.8 より大きければほぼ d を加えます。キーの出力は ρ = κ·h で、正確には比 (κ·h) ÷ (κc·h) ですが、κc·h はほぼ 1 です。キーは、変えたい場面の応答のある区間では出力がほぼ 1、それ以外ではほぼ 0 になるベクトルで、線形プローブに近いものです。応答の中の位置は 0 から数え(位置 0 が最初のトークンを決める位置)、区間は位置 0、位置 1〜3、位置 4 以降の 3 つです。d は、その性格が隠れ状態に作る違いを表す方向ベクトル(ステアリングベクトル)に、倍率 α を掛けたものです。学習は行わず、システムプロンプトも使わず、できたものは通常の llama.cpp が読み込める LoRA のファイルです。これらの計算は、解説ページ「CHiPS と FiSH のしくみ」で小さな数値例を使って説明しています。

4B と 27B のモデルで、お礼や褒め言葉への応答の半分強が「ツンデレ」(嬉しいのに、そっけない言い方で照れを隠す性格)の口調になり、質問への応答は答えも口調も変わりませんでした。残ったことは 2 つです。

  • キーが誤検出していました。 関係のない場面として確かめていた入力は、どれも質問でした。2026-11-13 の確かめで、質問以外の入力(近況報告、誘い)40 件では、公開した変更の係数 g が 38〜40 件で 0.2 以上になり、7〜18 件の応答が目標の口調になりました。前編には、これについての訂正を載せています。
  • 再現できない性格がありました。 ほかの性格も試していましたが、保留にしていました。

この記事では、その 2 つと、途中で見つけたもう 1 つを扱います。決まった 1 トークンをモデルに出力させる、別の種類の方向ベクトルです。これを使うと、空の応答を返させることもできます。

測ったモデルは 2 つです。Qwen3.5-4B(Q8_0)と、Qwen3.8-27B を追加で学習させた Swift-1.5-Qwen3.8-27B の IQ2_XS のファイル(重み 1 つあたり約 2 ビット)です。最後の測定はどれも 1 回だけで、ことわりが無ければ貪欲法(各位置でロジットが最も高いトークンを選ぶ方法)で生成しました。入力・システムプロンプトの下書き・応答の分類は、文章にした定義をもとに Claude が書き、分類しました。人が分類したものはありません。この記事で 2 つの結果に「有意差なし」と言うときは、対応のある検定で有意な差が見つからなかったという意味で、同じだと示したという意味ではありません。

これまでのキーが誤検出した理由

キーは、対象の位置(目標の場面の応答の、ある区間。最初のトークン、位置 1〜3、それ以降のどれか)と、対象外の位置(ベクトルを加えたくない位置)での、モデルの隠れ状態から作ります。これまでの作り方は、対象の位置での隠れ状態の平均 μ から、対象外の位置を表すいくつかの方向の成分を取り除くものでした。

μ=(対象の位置での h の平均)k=μ − Q QᵀμQ の列は、対象外の位置をまとめたベクトルの主な方向κ=k ÷ (μ·k)μ でのキーの出力が 1 になるように割る

対象外の位置をまとめたベクトルは、次の 3 種類です。

  • 目標の場面の応答ごとに、ほかの区間での h の平均
  • 変えたくない場面の応答ごとに、全位置での h の平均
  • 変えたくない場面の応答ごとに、最初の位置での h

Q には、これらのベクトルを特異値分解して得た方向のうち、累積寄与率が 99% に達するまでの方向だけを使います。この作り方を射影除去法と呼びます。対象外の位置をこのようにまとめてから使うので、キーの出力が対象外の位置 1 つ 1 つで 0 になるとは限りません。

変えたくない場面は、質問だけでした。そのため、キーが区別するようになったのは「お礼」ではなく「質問ではない入力」でした。近況報告や誘いを変えたくない場面に加えるのが素直な直し方ですが、それだけでは足りませんでした。射影除去法のままでは、開発用データの近況報告・誘い 40 件(前編の確かめとは別で、キーの構築に使っていない入力)での誤検出は、39 件から 22 件に減っただけでした。

最初に試した別の作り方も足りませんでした。27B のモデルでの、別の性格(短くそっけなく応答する)を使った予備の実験です。この実験では近況報告・誘いが目標の場面で、お礼・褒め言葉は変えたくない場面に入れてありました。キーを最小二乗法で解き、位置ごとに均等に数えました。質問での誤検出は止まりました(開発用データの質問 32 件で、これまでのキーは 8 件、新しいキーは 0 件)。しかし、お礼・褒め言葉には、まだベクトルを加えていました。この実験の開発用データの 8 件すべてで、位置 1〜3 ではなく位置 0 で加えていて、誤検出する位置が移っただけでした。この実験の最後の評価では、お礼・褒め言葉 15 件のすべてで、位置 0 の g が 0.90 以上になりました。

これらの入力の違いは、モデルの隠れ状態に表れています。応答を書き始める直前の隠れ状態に線形プローブを当てて入力の種類を予測すると、初めて見る入力の 98% で当たりました(2 つのモデルとも。くわしくは下の振り分けの節)。同じ測定で、キーと同じやり方(ある種類で 1、ほかで 0 を目標に、重みを付けて当てはめる)でも線形プローブを作りました。近い種類(お礼・褒め言葉、または近況報告・誘い)の重みを対象外の側の 1% に下げると、多くの層でそこに誤検出が出ました。問題はモデルが何を表しているかではなく、位置の重みの付け方でした。

  • モデルは 2 つです。 27B は追加で学習させたモデルで、約 2 ビットに量子化したものです。量子化していないモデルや、元の Qwen3.8-27B では測っていません。モデルの思考モード(thinking)は切ってあります。入れれば、システムプロンプトの側はもっと良くなるかもしれません。

再現

新しいキーは、gne v0.3.5 だけで作り直せます。構築用データの変えたくない場面を、入力の種類ごとに名前付きのグループに分け、グループごとに数十件を入れます。質問以外の入力(近況報告、誘い、挨拶、頼みごと、言いかけ・独り言)も入れます。そのうえで、次を実行します。

export GNE_MODEL=model.gguf
gne nw pipeline --contrast C.yaml --direction NAME --layers 11-23 --alpha 1,1,0.8 --key-fit ridge --save R.yaml
gne nw check  --recipe R.yaml --contrast CHECK.yaml     # CHECK.yaml: キーの構築に使っていない入力を、同じ名前のグループで
gne nw export --recipe R.yaml -o R.lora.gguf

gne nw check の最後に出る、グループごとの誤検出の表を見てください。層 11〜23 は Qwen3.5-4B 用の値です。

振り分けの実験で gne を使ったのは一部です。gne で行ったのは、レシピ(gne で変更をまとめて書く設定ファイル)のユニット(スイッチ 1 つ分の設定)、gne nw verify、gne nw check、gne eval、LoRA の書き出しと llama.cpp への読み込みです。自分たちのスクリプトで行ったのは、出力層の重み行列から作った方向ベクトル(gne にはまだそのためのコマンドが無く、gne のファイルの形式で保存しました)、振り分けのキー(--key-fit ridge ができる前に作ったもので、式は同じですが、行の間引き方が少し違い、正則化係数はスイッチごとに決めました)、層の探索、システムプロンプトの下書き、線形プローブ、数え上げです。gne nw pipeline --key-fit ridge で振り分けのキーがそのまま作り直せるかは、確かめていません。
