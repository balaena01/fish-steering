FiSH, part 2: keys that leak less, and sorting remarks with four rewritten neurons

The first article turned a few idle MLP neurons into switches that write a tone into replies in one kind of scene. Two problems were left: the switches also opened on remarks that are not questions, and some kinds of persona could not be written at all. Here we change how the keys are built (on the 4B model, the leak went from 81 of 105 prompts to 1), report what a direction could and could not carry, and give the same switches a different job: putting a marker at the start of the reply that says whether to stay silent, acknowledge, or answer. With four rewritten neurons, the marker was right for 94.2% and 93.8% of 400 new remarks on a 4B and a 27B model. An instruction chosen from 24 drafts got 92.5% and 96.0%, and we found no significant difference. With FiSH, most replies to remarks that should simply be answered began as they did without the edit (161 and 177 of 200), and nothing was added to the input; but many of the replies after its marker repeated the marker or looped (38% and 16%). Writing the end-of-reply token in place of the "no response" marker kept the accuracy (94.0% and 94.5%) and left nothing to break on that side. Answering only when called by name did not work well enough.

Where part 1 left off

Part 1 described FiSH. In a gated MLP, a neuron reads the layer input twice, through a gate row and an input row, and adds its output column to the residual stream in proportion to the product. FiSH takes two neurons that do very little, puts a key into their gate rows and a direction into their output columns, and makes the pair work as a switch: when the key responds below 0.4 nothing is written, and above 0.8 exactly the intended amount is written. The key is a vector that responds near 1 in the scene and stretch of the reply we want to change, and near 0 elsewhere. The direction is the difference a persona makes inside the model. There is no training and no system prompt, and the result is a LoRA for stock llama.cpp.

On a 4B and a 27B model, a little over half of the replies to thanks and compliments came out in a tsundere tone (a stock character who hides being pleased behind a brusque reply), and replies to questions kept their answers and their tone. Two things were left open:

  • The keys leaked. Every unrelated prompt we had tested was a question. On 40 remarks that are not questions (news, invitations), the published edits wrote on 38 to 40 of them, and 7 to 18 replies came out in the tone. Part 1 carries a correction about this.
  • Some personas could not be written. We had tried other characters and set them aside.

This article covers both, and a third thing we found along the way: a different kind of direction that writes one fixed token, which can also make the model say nothing.

Everything here was measured on two models: Qwen3.5-4B (Q8_0), and Swift-1.5-Qwen3.8-27B, a fine-tuned version of Qwen3.8-27B, in its IQ2_XS file (about 2 bits per weight). Each final test ran once, with greedy decoding unless noted. The prompts, the instruction drafts and the sorting of replies were written or done by instances of Claude, from written definitions; no human labelled anything. When we say two results are at the same level, we mean a paired test found no significant difference between them, not that they were shown to be equal.

Keys that leak less

Why the old keys leaked

A key is built from the model's internal states at the places where we want to write (replies in the scene, at one stretch: the first token, tokens 1 to 3, or the rest) and at the places where we do not. The old formula takes the average state at the places to write and removes from it the parts along a few directions that describe the places not to write. Those directions are taken from one average per reply (over the other stretches of the target replies, and over every position of each reply in the scenes to leave alone) and from the first position of each reply in the scenes to leave alone, and only the directions that make up 99% of their sum of squares are kept. Single positions are not checked one by one.

The places not to write had been replies to questions only. So the keys learned "a remark that is not a question", not "thanks". Adding news and invitations to the places not to write was the obvious fix, and it was not enough: with the old formula, the leak on 40 unseen news and invitation prompts went from 39 to 22.

Our first replacement also fell short. On the 27B model, with another persona (short, terse replies to news and invitations), we solved for keys by least squares and counted every reply position once. The leak into questions stopped (8 of 32 search questions with the old keys, 0 with the new ones), but thanks and compliments, which were among the places not to write, were still written on: all 8 in the search set, now at the first token of the reply instead of tokens 1 to 3. In the final test, all 15 thanks and compliments got 0.90 or more of the intended amount at the first token.

The difference between these remarks is there in the model's internal state. From its state just before the reply starts, a linear read-out names the kind of remark correctly for 98% of unseen remarks, on both models (more in the routing section below). In the same measurement, lowering the weight of the neighboring kinds (thanks and compliments, or news and invitations) to 1% of the "do not write" side made the read-out leak into them on many layers. The problem was how the places were weighted, not what the model knows.

The change

Two things changed, and only the keys. The direction, layers, strengths and neurons of the published 4B edit stayed as they were.

  • The key is solved for directly. It is the vector that returns 1 at the places to write and 0 at the places not to write, in the least-squares sense, with a ridge penalty. The penalty strength is chosen from 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10 and 100 by splitting the building prompts into five parts. No gradient training.
  • Every kind of place gets the same say. The places not to write are split into combinations of a kind of remark (each named group of prompts to leave alone, plus the other stretches of the target replies) and a stretch of the reply (first token, tokens 1 to 3, the rest). Each combination carries the same total weight. Counting each position once, long explanations fill the "do not write" side, and "the first token of replies to news" makes up less than 1% of it.

The keys were built from 576 prompts: 120 thanks and compliments, and 456 to leave alone in eleven groups (news 96, invitations 24, greetings 50, requests 50, muttering or trailing off 50, and questions of six kinds). Both stages were rebuilt (keys from the unedited model, then again with the edit applied, as in part 1). Before looking at any reply, we fixed a rule for picking the form (few target prompts left unwritten, few news and invitations written on, and the smaller sum of the two), and compared the forms on 157 search prompts the keys had not seen. A fifth form, which also recalibrated the key's response from its distribution, gave the same counts and was not used.

The chosen form was then tested once on prompts that no key had seen: 100 thanks and compliments, 105 remarks that are not questions (60 new news and invitations, 15 each of greetings, requests and muttering), and 130 questions.

  • The tone in the target scene did not drop. With greedy decoding it rose from 57 to 70 (p = 0.04); with sampling, 58 to 63 (not significant). "I managed to get up early this morning." got "Hah... getting up early, of all things..." from the published edit. With the new keys it got the unedited model's reply, character for character: "That's wonderful! Getting up early is a great way to start the day..."

Reproducing it with gne

The measurement was made with scripts outside gne and then added to gne v0.3.5 as an option, --key-fit ridge. Running gne nw pipeline --direction NAME --key-fit ridge with the same 576 prompts, direction, layers and strengths rebuilt the keys of the measurement: the three first-stage keys were identical in every element, and of the three second-stage keys two were identical and one differed by 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ of its largest element. The 157 search replies and the per-prompt amounts were the same. The run took 20.7 minutes on an RTX 3080 with the CPU.

gne nw check now prints a table of leaks per group of prompts to leave alone: for each group, how many prompts got 0.2 or more of the intended amount at some stretch, at each stretch, and at the first token alone. A leak into one kind of remark is diluted in the overall numbers, and a change of formula can move a leak to the first token without removing it; the table shows both. The default way of building keys is unchanged; the new one is chosen with --key-fit ridge.

What this does not show

One model, one persona, one direction, one run. The readers' definitions were not checked again on these replies. The remarks tested were of the same five kinds that the keys were shown (the prompts themselves were different). Kinds the keys were not shown, such as complaints, jokes, long pasted text, or remarks later in a conversation, were not tested. The 27B edits and the second persona of part 1 were not rebuilt this way, and no key built with shuffled labels was compared. The published LoRAs of part 1 are still the earlier edits.

What a direction can carry

A key decides where to write. The direction decides what. Part 1 built the direction by having the model read the same reply twice, once with the persona's instruction and once without, and averaging the difference in the layer outputs. Reading two different replies adds the difference between their words, and for the tsundere tone that alone sent replies into loops.

We tried this way of building a direction on four more personas on the 27B model, adding the direction straight to the layer outputs (no neurons, no keys) on 40 search prompts of each persona's scene. Replies were read blind against the replies to the instruction itself.

  • A short, plain tone was carried; set phrases and fixed vocabulary were not. The terse persona came out in 24 of 40 replies, as short replies without polite forms, but not as the instruction's own one-word replies ("...I see", "...if you like"). The refined lady came out in none, and the grandiose persona in at most 2 (5 with sampling). At the highest strength, 22 of the grandiose replies had a posturing tone without the vocabulary. (The instruction itself wrote the refined lady's speech in only 21 of 40; in 17 more it used the ending once and was plainly polite otherwise.)
  • Content that depends on what the user said was not carried. The jealous persona came out in at most 2 of 40. What came out was a friendly, affectionate tone (12 to 22 replies), without jealousy, possessiveness or asking the user to stay.
  • Reading two different replies carried the content, with damage. A direction from replies generated under each condition gave 20 and 23 jealous replies of 40 (two seeds, sampling at temperature 1.0 with a presence penalty), against 36 and 38 for the instruction. But 11 and 10 replies did not fit the prompt, 20% to 42% of the direction's replies used a male first-person pronoun although none of the replies it was built from did, and in some the speaker swapped roles with the user. With greedy decoding, 18 to 26 of 40 fell into repetition. Making the strength larger did not raise the count. Splitting the direction into a tone part and a jealousy part did not help either (12 and 17 jealous, 10 and 12 that went wrong).
  • A fixed first character could not be written this way. For the sorting task below, we built the same-reply direction from an instruction to begin the reply with "■" and added it at the first position of the reply. At three strengths, 0 of 450 replies began with the marker; the wording changed, the first character did not.

Our reading, which we did not test further: an average difference carries what appears in every reply in about the same shape (length, politeness, a flustered cover). It does not carry a fixed word, or words chosen in response to who and where the user mentioned. For the content we have no fix. For a fixed word we found one, below.

Writing one fixed token

The model's last step turns its final state into a score for every token, by multiplying with the output matrix. Each token has a row in that matrix, and adding that row to the state raises that token's score directly. So we used the row for "■" (and for "◇") as the direction, added at the first position of the reply only. Both markers are single tokens in these models. We set the strength as the score increase we were aiming for, and converted that to a vector length; the conversion is an estimate (see “The current form, in one place”).

Before any neuron was rewritten, we measured where to write it, without generating replies. On the 4B model we tried every layer from 1 to 30 one at a time, runs of 4 and 8 layers, and layers 11 to 23, each with four small variants of the row. On the 27B model we tried every even layer from 2 to 62 and runs of layers, and then the neighbors of the provisional choice. For each candidate we raised the strength until the marker was the top token for 95% of the search remarks, and then measured how much the next 8 tokens changed when the marker was placed first (a KL divergence, in nats; small means the reply carries on as it would have). The rule for choosing was fixed beforehand: the smallest change in the next tokens, counting everything within 0.01 of the smallest as tied; then the smallest strength; then the fewest layers; then the plain row before its variants; then the shallower layer.

  • Every layer worked. All 156 candidates on the 4B model, and all on the 27B model, made the marker the top token for 95% of the remarks at some strength. We had expected that only layers near the output would work; that was not so.
  • Deeper layers were better on both counts. Shallow layers needed a much larger push (at the shallowest layer, the vector was about 15 to 22 times as long as the state it was added to) and changed the following tokens more: from 0.22 at layer 1 to 0.0004 at layer 30 on the 4B model, and from 0.28 at layer 2 to 0.0002 at layer 62 on the 27B model.
  • Several layers were no better than one. Runs of 4, 8 or 13 layers did not beat a single layer at the same depth.
  • The rule chose layers 22 and 25 on the 4B model (strengths 24 and 32) and layers 51 and 57 on the 27B model (24 for both). The deeper layers in the tie band differed by less than 0.01 nat.

Added straight to the layer outputs with greedy decoding, the marker came first in 30 of 30 and 118 of 120 search remarks on the 4B model, and 30 of 30 and 115 of 120 on the 27B model. But the model often took the marker as a bullet point and kept using it: 43% and 23% of those replies on the 4B model, 13% and 4% on the 27B model. One step weaker, the marker dropped sharply (0 of 30 for "■" on both models, 68 of 120 for "◇" on the 4B model). The usable range of strength is narrow.

Sorting remarks with the switches

The kind of remark can be read from the model's state

We wrote 800 short Japanese remarks of ten kinds, balanced for length, topic and politeness. From the state just before the reply starts, a linear read-out (a weighted sum of that state, the same shape as a key) named the kind correctly for 98% of 300 unseen remarks on both models (95% intervals 96 to 99), and the three groups below for 98% and 99%. Two caveats. The characters alone also tell a lot: counting which characters and pairs of characters occur gets 92%, because each kind has its own words ("thank you", "let's"). Where the internal state does better is across politeness: trained on polite remarks and tested on plain ones (and the reverse), the state got 95 to 97% and the characters 74 to 79%. And reading a fact out of a state does not show that the model uses it.

The task

The program around a model sometimes needs to decide, before the reply is read, whether to say nothing, give a short acknowledgement, or answer. We asked the model to say so with the first character of its reply:

The grouping is our own choice. A reply was judged by its first character after stripping spaces, mechanically; no reader was involved.

The edit

Two switches, two neurons each: four neurons in the whole model. Each switch writes the output-matrix row of its marker at the first position of the reply, at the layer and strength chosen above. Its key is a least-squares key with the same weight for each combination of kind and stretch, responding only at the first position, built from 1,000 remarks (100 of each kind) in two stages. The places not to write include every later position of every reply, so that the switch does not open again after the marker. On 300 search remarks it sorted 283 (4B) and 286 (27B) correctly, and nothing was changed after that.

How empty replies were counted

We changed one rule after seeing results, and both counts are given here. At first, a reply whose first character was neither marker counted as "answer". In the first round of drafts on the 27B model, the top draft had 31 empty replies (the model ended the reply at once), all on remarks that should be answered, and all were counted as correct. After seeing that, we decided to count an empty reply as wrong, for every method, both when choosing drafts and in the final test.

With the original rule: nothing changes on the 4B model (no method gave an empty reply). On the 27B model the chosen instruction gets 385 instead of 384 (96.2%; 18 against 8, p = 0.076), and a different draft would have been chosen; it scored 384 (96.0%; p = 0.12 against FiSH). FiSH gave no empty replies. The verdict is the same under both rules.

Beyond accuracy

We had decided beforehand to report these separately rather than fold them into one score.

  • FiSH mostly leaves the answers alone. Of the 200 remarks that should be answered, 161 (4B) and 177 (27B) got replies whose first 30 characters match the unedited model's (68 and 103 were identical throughout), and FiSH put a marker on 0 and 1 of them. With the chosen instruction, none of the 200 started like the unedited reply: it makes the model answer briefly ("Soybeans." where the unedited model explains).
  • FiSH adds nothing to the input. The chosen drafts add 2,525 and 1,729 tokens to every conversation (the original text added 199).
  • FiSH writes only the marker; what follows is the model's ordinary reply, a long one (median 118 and 115.5 tokens), and often broken: 68 of 181 marker replies on the 4B model and 29 of 182 on the 27B model used the marker again or fell into repetition. After the instruction's marker came a short acknowledgement (median 11 and 9 tokens) and no such breakage.
  • Fact questions (40): no edit 34, instruction 31, FiSH 34 on the 4B model; 37, 34 and 36 on the 27B model. Some of the instruction's short answers were simply wrong; on the other hand, our check counts a reply as correct when the right word appears anywhere in it, which favors long replies.
  • Where each one misses. The chosen instruction put "■" on short requests for advice on the 4B model (8 of 40) and treated polite trailing-off as news on the 27B model (7 of 40). FiSH missed mostly on greetings that ask something ("Hey, is it snowing over there?"), usually answering them without a marker: 10 of 40 greetings on the 4B model (9 without a marker), 15 of 40 on the 27B model (all without a marker).
  • Two things we measured only in the first round. Together with another system prompt that gives the model a role (an English teacher), FiSH fell to 81.8% and 86.5%, and only 19 and 17 of 40 trailing-off remarks got "■"; with a cooking-advice prompt it stayed at 98.2% and 95.2%. The chosen instructions were not tested with another prompt. And FiSH changed neither the general benchmark of gne eval (0.972 before and after, on both models) nor perplexity by more than 0.1%.

What this shows

With four rewritten neurons, the marker was right about as often as with an instruction chosen from 24 drafts. FiSH did that while most replies that should be answers began as before, without adding to the input, and with fact answers at about the unedited model's level (34 against 34, 36 against 37). But the replies after its marker break easily, and a marker by itself is neither a short acknowledgement nor silence: a program reading it has to strip the marker and cut or discard the rest. For "no response", the next section removes that step. And in one test FiSH did worse when the model had been given a role by another prompt.

Saying nothing with the end-of-reply token

A model ends its reply by writing a special token that closes its turn. If the "no response" switch writes the output-matrix row of that token instead of the row of "■", the reply should simply be empty, with nothing after it to go wrong. We tried this on both models, keeping the "◇" switch as it was.

The layer and strength were chosen the same way as for "■": every layer measured, and a rule fixed beforehand. A reply that ends at once has no continuation to compare, so this time the rule looked first at harm to the remarks that should not be silenced (how many of them would end at once if the key opened a little, then how much their first token changed), then at strength, number of layers and depth. On the 4B model it chose layer 22 with strength 32, the layer of the "■" switch, so we kept that switch's key and neurons and replaced only the direction. On the 27B model it chose layer 50 with strength 24; the earlier keys were built for other layers, so the key was rebuilt the same way as before. On the search remarks, 282 (4B) and 288 (27B) of 300 were sorted correctly, and 3 and 1 of 270 were wrongly silenced. We then ran it once on the same 400 new remarks as in the comparison above. They had been used once there, and nothing in this round was chosen on them.

Answering only when called by name: not good enough

We also tried a stricter version on the 4B model: answer only when the user calls the assistant by a name we chose, "Soyoka" (ソヨカ), and stay silent otherwise. The silent cases included remarks with no name, remarks to someone else, names that look similar, talking about Soyoka in the third person, and words that contain the name ("the Soyoka shopping street"). We picked a name unlikely to be mistaken for ordinary words; the model splits it into three tokens. The edit used the same end-of-reply direction.

The rule was changed after seeing a result. With the layer and strength chosen for trailing-off remarks, adding the direction directly silenced 52 of 68 search remarks that should be silent; the rule fixed beforehand (90%) would have stopped the experiment there. Of the 16 that were not silenced, 12 were plain questions and requests with no name, and 4 mentioned Soyoka without calling her. We then chose the layer and strength again over all layers on this task's search remarks (layer 24, strength 48), and continued. A finished sentence, especially a question, seems to pull the model toward answering even when it is not addressed, so silencing it takes a stronger push. That is our reading; what we measured is that the strength needed rose from 32 to 48, and that the remarks which resisted were clear questions and requests.

On 224 new remarks, run once, it stayed silent on 133 of the 136 where it should (all 72 with no name, all of the other-name, third-person and contains-the-name remarks, and 13 of 16 similar names). But it also went silent on 22 of the 88 remarks that did call it by name (25%): 9 of 24 with the name first, 6 of 24 after "hey", 7 of 24 with the name last; it answered all 16 that were the name alone. On the search remarks it had been 10 of 44, and in second turns it answered only 4 of 20 remarks that called it. Our rule, fixed beforehand, calls anything above 5% wrongly silenced not practical, so this is not practical. The key could not tell well enough whether the name was being used to call: on held-out building remarks, it half-opened on 44% to 77% of the remarks that did call the name.

Six instruction drafts, written by two separate instances of Claude, could hardly get the 4B model to give an empty reply (at most 1 of the 68 search remarks that should be silent). The chosen draft was right on 91 of the 224 new remarks, about the same as doing nothing (88). FiSH's 199 is higher, but against an instruction that did not work, so we do not count it as a win. The general benchmark of gne eval fell from 0.972 to 0.833 with this edit, because its questions do not contain the name and were silenced as designed; we did not measure it with the name added.

The current form, in one place

The pair of neurons is the same as in part 1. What changed is how the key is computed and, for sorting remarks and for silence, what the output column holds.

neuron 1:  gate ← s(κ − 0.4·κ1)    input ← κ1    output ← +d ÷ (0.4·s)
neuron 2:  gate ← s(κ − 0.8·κ1)    input ← κ1    output ← −d ÷ (0.4·s)

key κ (new): κ̂ = (G + λI)⁻¹ b over every reply position x_i with weight w_i
             G = Σ w_i x_i x_iᵀ,  b = Σ_write w_i x_i,  λ = c × (mean of the diagonal of G)
             κ = κ̂ ÷ (κ̂ · m)
             weights: places to write sum to 1; places to leave sum to 1, equal over every
             combination of a kind of remark and a stretch of the reply

d for a tone:    α × (the difference the persona's instruction makes, the same reply read twice)
d for a marker:  β × u ÷ ‖u‖,  u = the row of the output matrix for the marker
                 β = Δ × r̄ ÷ ((γ ⊙ u) · u ÷ ‖u‖)
                 Δ = the score increase aimed for (24 or 32; an estimate, see below),
                 r̄ = the median rms of the last layer's output at the first position of the
                 reply (before the final normalization), γ = the weights of the final normalization
d for silence:   the same, with u = the row of the end-of-turn token
                 (Δ = 32 on the 4B model, 24 on the 27B model)

κ1 is the constant key (about 1 everywhere) and s the gate scale, as in part 1. Rows are thinned per reply: the first positions are all kept, and a sample of the later ones carries their weight. gne nw check reports the key response of a pair as κ·h ÷ κ1·h, the value compared with 0.4 and 0.8.

Δ is an estimate: the conversion ignores the layers after the one written to and the change in rms. Measured at the chosen layers and strengths, on the search remarks where the switch should open, the score rose by 51% to 86% of Δ on average (at layer 22 of the 4B model, by 14.2 for "■" at Δ 24 and by 16.9 for the end-of-turn token at Δ 32).

The symbols are not the same in every article: here s is the gate scale of the neurons, while in the CHiPS article s is the strength of a head; and the CHiPS article uses r for a unit direction, so the size of the final state is written r̄ here.

Limits

  • Two models. The 27B model is a fine-tuned version, quantized to about 2 bits. The unquantized model and the original Qwen3.8-27B were not tested. The models' thinking mode was off; with it on, instructions might do better.
  • One run each, greedy. 40 remarks per kind; one remark moves a kind by 2.5 points. "Same level" means no significant difference was found.
  • Claude on both sides. The instruction drafts, the new remarks and their correct groups were all written by instances of Claude, and no human checked them. Shared assumptions about what "trailing off" or "advice" looks like may have helped the instructions, or both methods.
  • The new keys: one model, one persona, and only the five kinds of remark they were shown.
  • What did not work: many replies after a marker repeat it or loop (for "no response" the end-of-reply token removes this, for "acknowledge" it remains); the usable strength is narrow; another role prompt lowered the sorting; FiSH did not reach the read-out of the internal state; answering only when called by name was not practical; and the refined lady, the grandiose boy and the jealous persona were not carried.
  • The routing keys were built without any system prompt, and only on first turns of a conversation. In small tests of second turns, the silence switch silenced 1 of 4 trailing-off remarks, and the name switch answered 4 of 20 remarks that called it.
  • Answering by name: one model, one name, and the layer and strength were chosen again after a result (see above).

What we will try next

  • Stop the marker from turning into bullet points: a second switch at the position right after the marker.
  • For "acknowledge briefly", end the reply after a short acknowledgement: a second switch that writes the end-of-reply token a few tokens after "◇".
  • Build the routing keys with the role prompt in place, and with remarks from later turns of a conversation.
  • For answering by name, read whether the name is used to call at the name's own position and carry that to the start of the reply, or use a name that is a single token.
  • Try the same on a larger model without heavy quantization.
  • Add the output-matrix direction to gne as a command, and check whether --key-fit ridge rebuilds the routing keys.
  • For personas whose words depend on what the user said, an average direction is not enough. We do not have a plan yet.

Reproduce

The new keys can be rebuilt with gne v0.3.5 alone. Write the contrast set with the scenes to leave alone in named groups, one group per kind of remark, several dozen prompts each, including remarks that are not questions (news, invitations, greetings, requests, trailing off). Then:

export GNE_MODEL=model.gguf
gne nw pipeline --contrast C.yaml --direction NAME --layers 11-23 --alpha 1,1,0.8 --key-fit ridge --save R.yaml
gne nw check  --recipe R.yaml --contrast CHECK.yaml     # CHECK.yaml: unseen prompts, the same named groups
gne nw export --recipe R.yaml -o R.lora.gguf

Read the table of leaks per group at the end of gne nw check. Layers 11 to 23 are for Qwen3.5-4B only.

The sorting experiment used gne for part of the work only. Done with gne: the recipe units, gne nw verify, gne nw check, gne eval, the LoRA, and loading it into llama.cpp. Done with our own scripts: the direction from the output matrix (gne has no command for it yet; we saved it in gne's file format), the routing keys (built before --key-fit ridge existed, with the same formula, but with rows thinned slightly differently and the penalty chosen for each switch), the layer search, the instruction drafts, the read-out, and the counting. Whether gne nw pipeline --key-fit ridge rebuilds the routing keys exactly has not been checked.

Silence and answering by name ran on gne v0.4.0 and were split the same way: the units, gne nw verify, gne nw check, gne eval and the LoRA with gne; the direction from the end-of-turn token's row, the keys (on the 4B model, the "■" key reused), the layer search, the instruction drafts and the counting with our own scripts.

The prompt sets, every reply, the readers' labels, the instruction drafts and the units of this article are in the repository (case4/ for the new keys, case5/ for sorting remarks and silence, case6/ for answering by name), with part2/recount.py, which recounts the main numbers from those files. 1 本目で残ったこと

1 本目では FiSH を説明しました。ゲートつきの MLP のニューロンは、層の入力をゲートの行と入力の行の 2 通りで読み、その積の分だけ出力の列を残差に足します。FiSH は、ほとんど働いていないニューロンを 2 個選び、ゲートの行に鍵を、出力の列に方向を入れて、2 個で 1 つのスイッチにします。鍵の反応が 0.4 より下なら何も書かず、0.8 より上なら狙った量ちょうどを書きます。鍵は、変えたい場面の返事の、ある区間ではほぼ 1、それ以外ではほぼ 0 を返すベクトルです。方向は、その性格がモデルの内部に作る違いです。学習は使わず、system プロンプトも付けず、できたものは普通の llama.cpp で動く LoRA です。

4B と 27B のモデルで、お礼や褒め言葉への返事の半分強が「ツンデレ」(嬉しいのに、そっけない言い方で照れを隠す性格)の口調になり、質問への返事は答えも口調も変わりませんでした。残ったことは 2 つです。

  • 書けない性格がありました。 ほかの性格も試していて、保留にしていました。

この記事は、その 2 つと、途中で見つけたもう 1 つ(決まった 1 トークンを書く、別の種類の方向。モデルを黙らせることもできる)を扱います。

測ったモデルは 2 つです。Qwen3.5-4B(Q8_0)と、Qwen3.8-27B に追加で学習させた Swift-1.5-Qwen3.8-27B の IQ2_XS のファイル(重み 1 つあたり約 2 ビット)です。最後の測定はどれも 1 回だけで、ことわりが無ければ greedy です。質問・指示文の下書き・返事の分類は、書かれた定義をもとに Claude が書き、分けました。人が分類したものはありません。この記事で 2 つの結果を「同じ水準」と言うときは、対の検定で有意な差が見つからなかったという意味で、同じだと示したという意味ではありません。

漏れの少ない鍵

いままでの鍵が漏れた理由

鍵は、書き込みたい位置(狙う場面の返事の、ある区間。最初のトークン、位置 1〜3、それ以降のどれか)と、書き込みたくない位置での、モデルの内部の状態から作ります。いままでの式は、書き込みたい位置の状態の平均から、書き込みたくない側を表すいくつかの向きの成分を取り除くものでした。その向きの材料は、返事 1 本ごとの平均(狙う場面の返事はほかの区間の平均、変えたくない場面の返事は全部の位置の平均)と、変えたくない場面の返事の最初の位置で、2 乗の和の 99% に届くまでの向きだけを使います。位置を 1 つずつは見ていません。

書き込みたくない位置は、質問への返事だけでした。そのため鍵が覚えたのは「お礼」ではなく「質問でない発言」でした。近況の報告や誘いを書き込みたくない側に足すのが素直な直し方ですが、それだけでは足りませんでした。いままでの式のままでは、鍵が見ていない近況の報告・誘い 40 問への漏れは、39 問から 22 問に減っただけでした。

最初に試した別の作り方も足りませんでした。27B のモデルで、別の性格(近況の報告・誘いに、短くそっけなく返す)を使い、鍵を最小二乗で解いて、返事の位置を 1 つずつ同じ重みで数えました。質問への漏れは止まりました(探索用の質問 32 問で、いままでの鍵は 8 問、新しい鍵は 0 問)。しかし、書き込みたくない側に入れていたお礼・褒め言葉には、まだ書いていました。探索用の 8 問の全部で、位置 1〜3 ではなく返事の最初のトークンに書いていて、漏れる場所が移っただけでした。最後の評価では、お礼・褒め言葉 15 問の全部で、最初のトークンに狙った量の 0.90 以上を書いていました。

これらの発言の違いは、モデルの内部の状態に表れています。返事を書き始める直前の状態から、線形の読み出しで発言の種類を当てると、初めて見る発言の 98% で当たりました(2 つのモデルとも。くわしくは下の振り分けの節)。同じ測定で、近い種類(お礼・褒め言葉、または近況の報告・誘い)の重みを「書かない側」の 1% に落とすと、多くの層で読み出しがそこに漏れました。問題はモデルが知っていることではなく、位置の重みの付け方でした。

変えたこと

変えたのは 2 点で、鍵だけです。公開した 4B の編集の方向・層・強さ・ニューロンは、そのままにしました。

  • 鍵を、直接解いて求める。 書き込みたい位置で 1、書き込みたくない位置で 0 を返すベクトルを、正則化つきの最小二乗で求めます。正則化の強さは 0.01・0.1・1・10・100 から、鍵を作る質問を 5 つに分けた確かめで決めます。勾配による学習は使いません。
  • どの種類の位置にも、同じ重みを持たせる。 書き込みたくない位置を、発言の種類(変えたくない場面の名前つきの群の 1 つずつと、狙う場面の返事のほかの区間)と返事の区間(最初のトークン、位置 1〜3、それ以降)の組み合わせに分け、どの組み合わせも重みの合計を同じにします。位置を 1 つずつ数えると、長い説明の返事が「書かない側」を埋め、「近況の報告への返事の最初のトークン」は、その 1% にも届きません。

κ  = κ̂ ÷ (κ̂ · m)            m = 書き込みたい位置の状態の、重みつきの平均

ニューロン 2 個の組は 1 本目と同じです。変わったのは鍵の計算と、振り分けと黙るために出力の列に入れるものです。

黙る d:     同じ式で、u = 自分の番を閉じるトークンの行(Δ は 4B で 32、27B で 24)

κ1 はどこでもほぼ 1 を返す定数の鍵、s はゲートの倍率で、1 本目と同じです。行は返事ごとに間引き、最初のほうの位置は全部残し、後ろの位置は一部を選んで、その分の重みを付けます。gne nw check が出す 2 個組の鍵の反応は κ·h ÷ κ1·h で、これを 0.4・0.8 と比べます。

Δ は見積もりです。書き込む層より後ろの層と、rms の変化を無視して換算しています。選んだ層と強さで、スイッチが開くべき探索用の発言について実際に測ると、点数の上げ幅は平均で Δ の 51〜86% でした(4B の層 22 では、「■」が強さ 24 で 14.2、自分の番を閉じるトークンが強さ 32 で 16.9)。

記号の意味は記事によって違います。ここでの s はニューロンのゲートの倍率で、CHiPS の記事の s はヘッドの強さです。また CHiPS の記事では r を長さ 1 の方向に使っているので、ここでは最終の状態の大きさを r̄ と書きました。
